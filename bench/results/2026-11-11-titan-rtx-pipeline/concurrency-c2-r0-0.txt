For millennia, the coastline has been a place of both opportunity and peril. Ships navigating the dark waters near landmasses faced the constant threat of running aground on hidden rocks or shallow reefs. To mitigate this danger, humanity developed one of its most enduring navigational aids: the lighthouse. The history of these structures is not merely a chronicle of architectural evolution but also a testament to the advancement of optical science, particularly through the invention of the Fresnel lens, which transformed how light was projected across vast distances.

The earliest precursors to modern lighthouses were simple bonfires lit on high cliffs or headlands. Ancient civilizations, including the Egyptians and Greeks, used these fires to guide mariners, but they were inefficient and often unreliable due to weather conditions. The first true lighthouse is widely considered to be the Pharos of Alexandria, built in the third century BC. Standing over one hundred meters tall, it utilized a large fire at its summit, with mirrors or polished surfaces potentially used to reflect and amplify the light. However, for centuries following the decline of the Roman Empire, lighthouse technology stagnated.
