For millennia, the coastline has been a place of both opportunity and peril. Ships laden with goods or carrying passengers across vast oceans relied on the stars for navigation, but when darkness fell or fog rolled in, the sea became a treacherous maze of hidden rocks and shifting currents. The solution to this ancient problem was deceptively simple: a light placed high above the water, visible from miles away. Yet, the history of lighthouses is not merely a story of fire and stone; it is a narrative of optical innovation, engineering ambition, and the relentless human desire to tame the unpredictable nature of the sea.

The earliest beacons were crude by modern standards. In ancient times, fires were lit on hilltops or towers, often fueled by wood or coal. These primitive lights served their purpose for coastal communities but lacked the intensity and consistency required for safe maritime navigation over long distances. The Romans built some of the first permanent lighthouse structures, such as the famous Pharos of Alexandria, which stood as a marvel of ancient engineering. However, these early towers relied on open flames that were easily extinguished by wind or rain, and their light dispersed in all directions, wasting energy and limiting visibility.
