For millennia, the coastline has been a place of both opportunity and peril. Ships navigating the dark waters near landmasses faced the constant threat of running aground on hidden rocks or shallow reefs. To mitigate this danger, humanity developed one of its most enduring maritime technologies: the lighthouse. The history of these structures is not merely a chronicle of architectural evolution but also a testament to the advancement of optical science, particularly through the invention of the Fresnel lens, which transformed how light was projected across vast distances.

The earliest precursors to modern lighthouses were simple bonfires lit on high cliffs or headlands. Ancient civilizations, including the Egyptians and Greeks, used these fires to guide sailors toward safe harbors. The most famous example from antiquity is the Pharos of Alexandria, built in the third century BCE. Standing over one hundred meters tall, it was considered one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World. However, these early structures relied on open flames, which were inefficient and difficult to maintain. The light produced was diffuse and scattered, meaning that only a small fraction of the energy generated actually reached distant ships.
