For millennia, the coastline has been a place of both opportunity and peril. Ships navigating the dark waters near landmasses faced the constant threat of running aground on hidden rocks or shallow reefs. To mitigate this danger, humanity developed one of its most enduring maritime technologies: the lighthouse. The history of these structures is not merely a chronicle of architectural evolution but also a testament to the advancement of optical science, particularly through the invention of the Fresnel lens, which transformed how light was projected across vast distances.

The earliest precursors to modern lighthouses were simple bonfires lit on high cliffs or headlands. Ancient civilizations, including the Egyptians and Greeks, used these fires as navigational aids, though they were often unreliable due to weather conditions and fuel shortages. The most famous example from antiquity was the Pharos of Alexandria, built in the third century BCE. Standing over one hundred meters tall, it utilized a large fire at its summit, with mirrors reportedly used to reflect the light out to sea. While impressive for its time, the Pharos represented a crude application of technology; the light source was diffuse, and the range was limited by the inefficiency of open flames and basic reflective surfaces.
