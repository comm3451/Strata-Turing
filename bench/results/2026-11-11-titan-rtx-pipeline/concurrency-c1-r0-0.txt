For millennia, the coastline has been a place of both wonder and terror. To sailors navigating the dark waters at night or through thick fog, the jagged rocks and hidden shoals represented an existential threat. The solution to this ancient problem was deceptively simple in concept but complex in execution: the lighthouse. While the idea of using fire to guide ships dates back to antiquity, the evolution of lighthouses is a story not just of architecture, but of optics, engineering, and the relentless human desire to tame the sea.

The earliest known lighthouses were essentially large bonfires built on high cliffs or islands. The Pharos of Alexandria, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, was a towering structure that used fire at its peak to guide ships into the harbor. However, these early beacons had significant limitations. Fire produced light in all directions, wasting energy and making it difficult for distant sailors to distinguish a lighthouse from other coastal fires or stars. Furthermore, the light was often obscured by smoke or weather. For centuries, progress remained slow, with many medieval lighthouses relying on coal fires or open flames that required constant tending and offered poor visibility over long distances.
