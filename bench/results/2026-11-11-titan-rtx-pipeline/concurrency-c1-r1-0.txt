The history of lighthouses is a narrative of humanity’s persistent struggle against the dark, a story that spans millennia and reflects our evolving understanding of optics, engineering, and maritime safety. From the earliest crude fires on hilltops to the sophisticated optical systems of the nineteenth century, lighthouses have served as silent sentinels, guiding sailors through treacherous waters and preventing countless disasters. Their evolution is not merely a tale of architectural change but a profound journey in the science of light itself.

In ancient times, the concept of a navigational aid was rudimentary. The most famous early example is the Pharos of Alexandria, built in the third century BCE, which stood as one of the tallest structures of its era. However, for centuries after its decline, maritime navigation relied on simple bonfires or torches placed at strategic points along coastlines. These early beacons were inefficient and dangerous; they required constant human attention to maintain the flame, and their light was often obscured by smoke or weather. The primary limitation was not just the fuel source but the lack of any mechanism to direct or amplify the light. A fire burns in all directions, wasting energy and failing to provide a focused beam that could be seen from great distances.
