For millennia, the coastline has been a place of both opportunity and peril. Ships laden with goods or carrying passengers relied on the sea for trade and travel, yet the jagged rocks and shifting sands that line many shores posed a constant threat to navigation. In this dangerous interface between land and water, the lighthouse emerged as one of humanity’s most vital technological achievements. The history of these structures is not merely a chronicle of stone towers rising from cliffs, but a narrative of evolving optical science, where the simple act of burning wood or oil was transformed into a precise instrument of safety through the mastery of light itself.

The earliest precursors to modern lighthouses were likely bonfires lit on high points along coastlines, dating back to ancient civilizations such as the Egyptians and Greeks. However, the first true lighthouse is widely considered to be the Pharos of Alexandria, built in the third century BCE. Standing over a hundred meters tall, this marvel of engineering guided ships into the harbor using a large fire at its summit. While effective for its time, these early structures relied on open flames that were easily obscured by fog or smoke and required constant manual maintenance. For centuries, lighthouse technology remained relatively static, with towers built from stone or brick housing simple oil lamps or candles
