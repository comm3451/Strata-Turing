The history of lighthouses is a narrative of humanity’s persistent struggle against the dark, a story that spans millennia and reflects our evolving understanding of optics, engineering, and maritime safety. From the crude bonfires on ancient cliffs to the intricate glass prisms of the nineteenth century, the evolution of these structures represents a continuous refinement of technology aimed at saving lives. The journey begins in antiquity, where the earliest known lighthouse, the Pharos of Alexandria, stood as a monumental beacon for sailors navigating the treacherous waters of the Mediterranean. Built around 280 BC, this towering structure used open fires to cast light across the sea. While effective for its time, these early methods were limited by the nature of fire itself; the light was diffuse, unstable, and easily obscured by wind or rain. For centuries, lighthouse keepers relied on wood, coal, or oil lamps, often supplemented by reflectors made of polished metal to direct the beam outward. However, these systems suffered from a fundamental flaw: they scattered light in all directions, wasting energy and reducing visibility for distant ships.
