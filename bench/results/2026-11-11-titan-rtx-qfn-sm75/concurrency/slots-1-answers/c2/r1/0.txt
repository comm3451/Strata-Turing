For millennia, the coastline has been a place of both opportunity and peril. Ships laden with goods or carrying passengers across vast oceans relied on the stars for navigation, but when darkness fell or fog rolled in, these celestial guides vanished, leaving mariners vulnerable to the jagged rocks and hidden shoals that claimed countless vessels. The solution to this ancient problem was not born overnight but evolved through centuries of trial, error, and technological innovation. The history of lighthouses is a story of humanity’s persistent desire to tame the sea, transforming simple fires into sophisticated optical instruments that could guide ships safely across thousands of miles of open water.

The earliest beacons were rudimentary affairs, often little more than large bonfires lit on high cliffs or prominent headlands. In ancient times, such as in Egypt and Greece, these fires served a dual purpose: they warned sailors of dangerous coastlines and guided them toward safe harbors. However, these early lights had significant limitations. They were difficult to maintain, easily extinguished by wind or rain, and their light was diffuse, spreading out in all directions rather than focusing on the horizon where it was needed most.
