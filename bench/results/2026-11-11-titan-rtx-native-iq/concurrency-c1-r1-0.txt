The history of lighthouses is a narrative of humanity’s persistent struggle against the dark, treacherous nature of the sea. For millennia, mariners relied on the stars and the sun for navigation, but when night fell or fog rolled in, these celestial guides vanished, leaving ships vulnerable to rocky shores and hidden reefs. The solution was artificial light, a technology that evolved from simple bonfires to complex optical systems capable of projecting beams across vast distances. This evolution reflects not only advancements in engineering but also a deepening understanding of physics and optics.

The earliest lighthouses were rudimentary structures designed to burn large fires on high cliffs or prominent headlands. The most famous example is the Pharos of Alexandria, built in the third century BC, which stood as a towering beacon for ancient sailors. These early lights were inefficient; they consumed enormous amounts of fuel and produced light that scattered in all directions, wasting energy and limiting visibility. For centuries, progress was slow. In medieval Europe, lighthouses were often maintained by religious orders or local communities who struggled to keep the flames burning consistently. The light source remained a simple open flame, whether from wood, coal, or oil, resulting in a weak, flickering glow that could be seen only at close range.
