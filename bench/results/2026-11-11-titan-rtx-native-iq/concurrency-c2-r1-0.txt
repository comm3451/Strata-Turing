For millennia, the coastline has been a place of profound danger. Ships navigating the dark waters near rocky shores or treacherous reefs relied on visual cues that were often obscured by fog, night, or storm. The lighthouse emerged as humanity’s most critical solution to this maritime peril, evolving from simple bonfires into complex optical engineering marvels. The history of these structures is not merely a chronicle of architectural progress but a testament to the development of optics and the human desire to impose order on the chaotic sea.

The earliest precursors to modern lighthouses were likely signal fires lit on high cliffs or prominent headlands. Ancient civilizations, including the Egyptians and Greeks, used such fires to guide vessels into harbors. The most famous example from antiquity was the Pharos of Alexandria, built in the third century BCE. Standing over one hundred meters tall, it utilized a large mirror at its apex to reflect sunlight during the day and firelight at night. While impressive, these early structures were inefficient. They relied on open flames that consumed vast amounts of fuel and produced light that scattered in all directions, wasting energy and limiting visibility range.
