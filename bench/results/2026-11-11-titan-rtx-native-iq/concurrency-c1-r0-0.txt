For millennia, the coastline has been a place of both opportunity and peril. Ships navigating the treacherous waters near landmasses have always relied on visual cues to avoid running aground, but before the advent of modern technology, these cues were often inadequate or nonexistent. The history of lighthouses is not merely a chronicle of architectural evolution; it is a testament to humanity’s persistent struggle against the darkness and the sea. From the crude bonfires of antiquity to the intricate optical engineering of the nineteenth century, the development of lighthouse technology represents one of the most significant advancements in maritime safety.

The earliest known lighthouses were simple structures designed to hold large fires. The Pharos of Alexandria, built in the third century BC, stands as the most famous example from this era. It was a towering structure that used open flames to guide ships into the harbor. However, these early beacons had severe limitations. Open fires produced light that was diffuse and easily obscured by fog or rain. Furthermore, the light source itself was unstable, requiring constant attention to maintain the flame. For centuries, progress in lighthouse technology remained stagnant.
