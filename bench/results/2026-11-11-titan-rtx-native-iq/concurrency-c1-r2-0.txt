The history of lighthouses is a narrative of humanity’s persistent struggle against the dark, a story that spans millennia and reflects our evolving understanding of optics, engineering, and maritime safety. From the earliest crude fires on hilltops to the sophisticated optical systems of the nineteenth century, lighthouses have served as silent sentinels, guiding sailors through treacherous waters and preventing countless disasters. The evolution of these structures is not merely architectural but deeply intertwined with the science of light itself, particularly the revolutionary work of Augustin-Jean Fresnel in the development of specialized lenses that transformed how light was projected across vast distances.

In ancient times, the concept of a lighthouse was rudimentary. The most famous early example was the Pharos of Alexandria, built in the third century BCE, which stood as one of the tallest structures of its era and used open fires to warn ships away from rocky shores. For centuries, however, most coastal warnings were simple bonfires or torches placed on high ground. These methods were inefficient and dangerous; they consumed vast amounts of fuel, produced inconsistent light, and often failed during storms.
