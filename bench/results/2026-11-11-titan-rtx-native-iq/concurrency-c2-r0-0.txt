For millennia, the coastline has been a place of both opportunity and peril. Ships laden with goods or carrying passengers across vast oceans relied on the stars for navigation, but when darkness fell or fog rolled in, these celestial guides vanished, leaving mariners vulnerable to the jagged rocks and hidden shoals that claimed countless vessels. The solution to this ancient problem was not a single invention, but a gradual evolution of technology and engineering known as the lighthouse. These structures stand as monuments to human ingenuity, representing a long journey from simple open fires to the sophisticated optical systems that define modern maritime safety.

The earliest precursors to the lighthouse were likely bonfires lit on high cliffs or headlands. In ancient times, communities would maintain these fires to warn ships away from dangerous shores or to guide them into harbors. However, these methods were inefficient and unreliable. A fire could be extinguished by wind or rain, and its light was diffuse, scattering in all directions rather than focusing on the horizon. The first true lighthouse is often attributed to the Pharos of Alexandria, built in the third century BC.
